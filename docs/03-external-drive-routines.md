# External-Drive Routines

The external drive is the most under-rated household appliance in the
twenty-first century. Costs less than two pizzas. Saves entire decades of
photos. Let's get you one, then build the ten-minute weekly habit.

## Buying: the three questions

**1. How big?** Look at how much space your data uses now (right-click the
folders you'd cry about → Properties/Get Info). Buy a drive at least twice
that size. For most families of normal photo-magnitude that means **1 TB**;
if you shoot lots of video, go **2 TB**. If the drive is too small, backups
become fiddly, and fiddly means abandoned.

**2. HDD or SSD?** Translated: the cheap spinning kind (HDD) has moving
parts and is a bit slower — but for a backup that sits in a drawer most of
the week, cheap-and-big wins. A solid-state drive (SSD) is faster and
tougher, great for small and mid-size archives. Either is fine. Don't let
this decision stall you — the worst drive is the drive you don't own.

**3. Cute or boring?** Boring-ish, but do look for a drive that works by
itself (most do): produces reasonable capacity for the price, comes with a
cable, and connects over USB. That's it. There's no wrong choice at this
level.

> One extra drive if you can: two cheap drives rotate — one in use, one
> somewhere else. See "Rotation" below.

## First setup: ten minutes

1. Plug the drive in.
2. If the computer offers to "set up Time Machine" (Mac) or asks to
   "format/initialize" — say yes for backups.

> Quick note on the word "format": it means "prepare the disk so your
> computer can use it," and yes, it erases what's there — which on a
> **brand-new drive** is nothing. Never format a drive that has your only
> copies on it. That path leads to the crying chapter.

3. Create one folder on the drive named `Backups`.

That's the whole setup. No software to buy, though your operating system's
built-in backup tool (Time Machine on Mac, File History on Windows) is
sitting right there and works well — recipes at the end of the book.

## The weekly rhythm

Here's the whole routine. It's deliberately boring.

**Every week (or whenever the guilt whispers):**

1. Plug the drive in.
2. Copy your treasure folder into `Backups`. (Recipe 1 walks every click
   and command; it takes minutes once the first copy is done.)
3. Wait for the copy to finish — watch for the progress bar, then go do
   something else.
4. Eject/safely remove the drive. Yanking the cable mid-write is how
   drives and files both get hurt, so the polite eject is not paperwork —
   it's protection.
5. Put the drive somewhere that is **not** sitting on top of the laptop.

Add a cheat if the weekly thing keeps not happening: pair it with something
you already do — water the plants, take the bins out, Sunday-pancakes. The
drive plugs in while the pancakes happen.

## Labeling, the low-tech superpower

A strip of masking tape on each drive, written by actual pen:

- `TREASURE A — last copied 2026-10-25`
- `TREASURE B — last copied 2025-08-14`

Update the tape every time you copy. This tiny ritual does three things:
tells you which drive is fresher, makes gaps obvious, and gives the date to
your once-a-year review. Ten seconds. No app.

## Rotation

If you have two drives, alternate: week 1 drive A gets copies, week 2 drive
B. First week one drives lives in the car trunk or a relative's closet —
t hat's your offsite copy, and it's already better than most humans'.

- **Keep drives away from heat, magnets... well, honestly, just away from
  sun, radiators, and speaker stacks.**
- **Never** the same bag as the laptop, never stacked in a damp basement
  corner.
- Desk drawer at work, relative's dresser, gym locker — all fine offsite
  homes.

> **Offsite paranoia, calibrated:** fires and floods are rare. But you
> only need one in a lifetime to make this habit worth it. The offsite
> copy is the lottery ticket that's actually worth owning.

## When copying gets fussy

- Drive says "full": start a new dated folder (`Backups-2026-10`) and delete
  nothing — or get a bigger drive. Old backups are history you can browse.
- Copy won't finish / keeps erroring: stop, don't force it. Check the cable,
  try another port. If it keeps happening, treat the drive as unreliable —
  buy a new one, copy everything to it, then decide about the old one (see
  after-a-crash chapter for the click-click noise warning).
- You dread the step: shrink what you're copying. Backing up 300 GB weekly
  is a hobby; backing up 12 GB is a chore of seconds.

## What NOT to do, learned the hard way

- Don't rely on one drive only. Three copies, remember?
- Don't keep the drive (or the only copy of your treasure) in a backpack
  that also has the laptop. Same tornado, same bag.
- Don't ever move data from your treasure to "free up space" and count the
  drive as the main home. Copy, don't move.
