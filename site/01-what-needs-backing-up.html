What Actually Needs Backing Up

Let's start with the burning-question test, because it's the fastest way to sort your files without a spreadsheet. If your house burned down tonight — rugs, couch, nothing happened to you, but the computer and drives are gone — what would you cry about, and what would you blush over having it gone?

Two different reactions, and both matter:

  • Cry about = irreplaceable. Must be backed up, starting today.
  • Blush over = painful to redo. Worth backing up once a routine exists.
  • Shrug about = everything else. You're allowed to ignore it. Really.

The cry-about list (back these up first)

  • Photos and videos — especially the only-copy-in-one-place ones: camera

rolls, scans of old prints, the baby years.

  • Your own writing — journals, the novel in progress, every document you

personally typed.

  • Paperwork that fades or burns — scans of IDs, leases, insurance

policies, diplomas, the signed thing from three lawyers ago.

  • Money records — tax returns, budgets, the spreadsheet that runs your

household. (Tax PDFs often have to be kept years; losing them causes real trouble.)

  • Contacts and messages you'd want back — the phone numbers you don't

know by heart anymore (that's all of them, right?).

  • Game saves and creative projects — worlds, music projects, drawing

files. Ask any kid — this one's huge.

The blush-over list (round two)

  • Browser bookmarks and the reading-list of shame
  • App settings, keyboard shortcuts, the layout you finally tuned
  • Downloads you actually still use — installers, fonts, manuals
  • That email folder full of receipts

The shrug-about list (ignore with pride)

  • The operating system itself. You can reinstall Windows or macOS. Don't

back it up.

  • Big programs and apps (games too). Media files you can re-download from

Steam, Netflix, Spotify, the App Store. Re-installing takes an hour; baby photos take forever... well, not the taking-photos part, but you get the idea — photos can't be re-lived.

  • Cache folders, temporary files, anything named ~temp.

The re-download test

For any file, ask one question: "Can I get this exact file back from somewhere else in five minutes?"

  • Photo from your 8th birthday → no → back it up.
  • Netflix movie → yes → don't back it up.
  • Tax return PDF → yes, if you can log into the government portal, but a

bit annoying and possibly time-limited → back it up.

  • The essay you wrote → no → back it up.
  • A 40 GB game → yes → don't.

If the answer is "yes, easily," skip it and save the drive space. This one test is how you keep backups small enough that you'll actually maintain them.

Wait — a sync folder is not automatically a backup

One friendly warning before we go on: if you put files in a syncing folder (the kind that mirrors to a cloud account), that's genuinely useful — but it's a mirror, not a history. Delete a file on your laptop, and the cloud copy usually gets deleted too, politely and instantly. A backup that deletes the file you're trying to save is a very polite way to lose data.

That's why backups and sync both exist, and it's exactly what the 3-2-1 rule in the next chapter untangles. If you only take one thing from this page: copies on more than one "thing," one of them offsite, at least one of them keeping versions from the past.

Do this today

Ten minutes, one action:

  1. Open the folder where your stuff lives (Documents, Photos, whatever).
  2. Make a beginner's treasure list: scan down it and jot the top ten

things you'd cry about losing. Paper or phone notes both fine.

  1. Star, or drag into one folder, the top three from that list.

Tomorrow, chapter 2 will tell you exactly what to do with that folder. (It's three copies, two kinds of thing, one elsewhere. You'll see.))
